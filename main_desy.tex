\begin{synopsis}
The polarization of the beam transmitted by a Si 111 phase plate at 2838 eV, measured at the P09 beamline of PETRA III, is simulated with the SHADOW4 ray tracing code. The agreement improves when the energy bandwidth selected by the monochromator is included.
\end{synopsis}

\begin{abstract}
	The crystal phase plate is a new optical component recently implemented in the SHADOW4 ray tracing code. It calculates the transmitted (forward-diffracted) beam of a perfect crystal in Bragg geometry, and transports the polarization of every ray using the Jones calculus. In this note we simulate the polarization of the beam transmitted by a \SI{10}{\micro\meter} thick Si 111 phase plate at 2838 eV, rocked around the Bragg angle, and compare it with measurements at the P09 beamline of PETRA III. The simulations are done for three input beams, to identify which characteristics of the incident beam are visible in the measurements: (i) an ideal monochromatic and collimated pencil beam, (ii) the monochromatic undulator beam, and (iii) a polychromatic undulator beam monochromatized by a Si 111 double-crystal monochromator. The energy bandwidth selected by the monochromator, whose equivalent angular spread is comparable to the Darwin width of the phase plate, is the main cause of the smoothing of the measured curves. A simple approximation, the convolution of the plane-wave Stokes parameters with a Gaussian of the same angular width, also reproduces the measurements. The simulations also give quantities that are not measured: the beam is partially polarized close to the Bragg angle, so that the circular polarization obtained from the measured linear polarization assuming a fully polarized beam is overestimated, and the handedness of the circular polarization is predicted.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------
% -----------------------------------------------------------------

X-ray phase plates (phase retarders) convert the linear polarization of the synchrotron beam into circular or rotated linear polarization. They are thin perfect crystal plates, set close to (but not exactly at) the Bragg angle and used in transmission: the transmitted (forward-diffracted) amplitudes $t_\sigma$ and $t_\pi$ for the $\sigma$ and $\pi$ polarizations (referred to the diffraction plane) have different phases, and a phase difference $\Delta\phi=\pm90^\circ$ (with similar amplitudes) converts a linear polarization at $45^\circ$ from the diffraction plane into circular polarization \cite{hirano1993nim,Detlefs2012,PolarizationDraft}. The P09 beamline of PETRA III (DESY) \cite{Strempfer2013} is equipped with a double phase-retarder setup \cite{Francoual2013}, with diamond plates for the energy range 3.2--14~keV and \SI{10}{\micro\meter} thin silicon plates for the energy range 2.7--3.2~keV \cite{Strempfer2013}.

In a companion paper \cite{SanchezdelRio2026efields} we have described the methods used in the ray tracing code SHADOW4 \cite{SHADOW4} to transport the electric fields of the rays and to modify them by the optical elements using the Jones calculus, in a way that conforms to the standard formalism of polarization optics \cite{PolarizationDraft}. In particular, a new optical element, the crystal phase plate, has been implemented to calculate the transmitted beam of a perfect crystal in Bragg geometry, and it has been validated against direct calculations of the transmission amplitudes with the {\it crystalpy} library \cite{Guigay2024} and against the two-beam dynamical theory far from the Bragg angle.

In this note we compare SHADOW4 simulations with the polarization of the beam transmitted by a Si 111 phase plate measured at the P09 beamline. At the photon energy of the measurements, 2838~eV, the Bragg angle of Si 111 is close to $45^\circ$, so that the plate behaves differently from a typical phase plate: close to the Bragg angle it also acts as a polarizer. The ray tracing makes it possible to include progressively the characteristics of the beam incident on the phase plate. We discuss three cases: (i) a pencil beam (a single ray), which is equivalent to the calculation with the transmission amplitudes for a plane monochromatic wave; (ii) the monochromatic beam emitted by the P09 undulator, which includes the angular distribution of the beam; and (iii) the polychromatic beam of the undulator after the Si 111 double-crystal monochromator of the beamline, which also includes the energy bandwidth. Finally, we compare these simulations with an approximated calculation, the convolution of the plane-wave result with a Gaussian distribution of angular deviations. The circular polarization was obtained from the measured linear polarization assuming a fully polarized beam, and its sign (the handedness) was not measured; the simulations give the degree of polarization of the beam and the handedness of the circular polarization.

The paper is organized as follows. The experimental setup is described in Sec.~\ref{sec:experiment}, and the simulation with SHADOW4 in Sec.~\ref{sec:simulation}. The results for the three incident beams are compared with the measurements in Sec.~\ref{sec:results}, and the approximated calculation by convolution is presented in Sec.~\ref{sec:convolution}. The discussion and conclusions are in Sec.~\ref{sec:conclusions}.

\section{Experimental setup}
\label{sec:experiment}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------
% -----------------------------------------------------------------

The source of P09 is a 2~m long U32 planar undulator (period 31.4~mm, 64 periods) placed in a high-$\beta$ straight section of PETRA III (electron energy 6.0~GeV, current 100~mA) \cite{Strempfer2013}. The beam emitted by the undulator is linearly polarized in the horizontal plane [Stokes vector $\mathbf{S}_{in}=(1,1,0,0)^T$]. The photon energy, 2838~eV, is in the first harmonic, close to the lowest energy available at the beamline (2.7~keV).

The phase plate is a Si 111 crystal plate of thickness \SI{10}{\micro\meter}, in symmetric Bragg geometry. The diffraction plane of the plate is rotated by $45^\circ$ around the beam with respect to the horizontal plane. Calculations with {\it crystalpy} \cite{Guigay2024} (script {\tt crystalpy\_desy\_p09.py}) give, at 2838~eV, a Bragg angle (corrected for refraction) $\theta_B=44.17^\circ$, and a Darwin half width for the $\sigma$ polarization $\omega_s=\SI{64.5}{\micro\radian}=13.3$~arcsec.

The Bragg angle close to $45^\circ$ means that the polarization factor for the $\pi$ polarization $C=\cos2\theta_B$ is close to zero. Therefore, within the Darwin width (angular range from about $-60$ to $+100$~\si{\micro\radian}), the $\sigma$ component (in the crystal frame) is diffracted while the $\pi$ component is not (Fig.~\ref{fig:crystalpy}a). 
Conversely, in the same range the transmitted beam is $\pi$ polarized (Fig.~\ref{fig:crystalpy}b).
Moreover, at this low photon energy the absorption of the plate is strong: the transmission far from the Bragg angle is only $\exp(-\mu t_c/\sin\theta_B)=0.022$, with $t_c$ the thickness (the transmittance of the $\pi$ component in Fig.~\ref{fig:crystalpy}b).

\begin{figure}
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{figures/crystalpyR.png}
\includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{figures/crystalpyT.png}
\caption{Si 111 perfect crystal plate of thickness \SI{10}{\micro\meter} in Bragg geometry, at 2838~eV, for the $\sigma$ and $\pi$ polarizations, versus the angle $\theta$ from the Bragg angle $\theta_B$ corrected for refraction. a) Reflectivity (reflected beam). b) Transmittance (transmitted or forward-diffracted beam).}
\label{fig:crystalpy}
\end{figure}

The normalized Stokes parameters $P_1=S_1/S_0$ (horizontal/vertical linear polarization) and $P_2=S_2/S_0$ (linear polarization at $\pm45^\circ$) of the transmitted beam were measured with the polarization analyzer of the beamline \cite{Strempfer2013} as a function of the angle of the crystal plate around the Bragg position (48 measurements between $-302$ and $+306$~arcsec). The circular polarization was obtained as $P_3=+\sqrt{1-P_1^2-P_2^2}$, assuming a fully polarized beam (degree of polarization DOP$=1$). Therefore, its sign (the handedness) is not measured, and its modulus is an upper bound of the circular polarization of a partially polarized beam.

\section{Simulation with SHADOW4}
\label{sec:simulation}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------
% -----------------------------------------------------------------

The phase plate is simulated with the SHADOW4 optical element {\tt S4PlanePhasePlateCrystal} \cite{SanchezdelRio2026efields}. For each ray, the transmission amplitudes $t_\sigma$ and $t_\pi$ are calculated with {\it crystalpy} \cite{Guigay2024} using the direction and photon energy of the ray, and the Jones matrix $\mathrm{diag}(t_\sigma,t_\pi)$ is applied to the electric field of the ray, with the $\sigma$ and $\pi$ directions defined by the diffraction plane. The direction of the ray is not changed.
The crystal is set at a glancing angle equal to the Bragg angle (corrected for refraction) for 2838~eV plus the detuning angle $\Delta\theta$. Therefore, a positive detuning $\Delta\theta>0$ increases the glancing angle of the beam on the plate.
The plate is rotated around the beam with the azimuthal angle $\alpha$ of the SHADOW4 element (see Fig.~4 in \cite{SanchezdelRio2026efields}). A positive $\alpha$ rotates the plate counter-clockwise for an observer at the source looking downstream, i.e., it is a left-handed rotation about the beam direction (opposite to the advance of a right-handed screw along the beam). The rotation angle $\chi$ of the standard formalism \cite{PolarizationDraft}, used in Sec.~\ref{sec:convolution}, is right-handed about the beam, and $\chi=-\alpha$.

The sense of the measured angle and of the plate rotation are not known a priori. We have therefore simulated the four combinations of $\alpha=\pm45^\circ$ and $\Delta\theta=\pm$(measured angle) for a pencil beam (Table~\ref{tab:combinations}). The Stokes parameters calculated by SHADOW4 for each ray refer to its $\sigma$ direction, which, after the phase plate, is the $\sigma$ direction of the diffraction plane
(for details see \cite{SanchezdelRio2026efields}). 
To compare with the measurements, done in the laboratory frame, an empty element rotated by $-\alpha$ is placed after the phase plate. 

The Stokes parameters of the beam are the averages over the $N$ traced rays (the lost rays do not contribute)
\begin{equation}\label{eq:stokes_sum}
S_k=\frac{1}{N}\sum_j S_k^{(j)}, \qquad k=0,\dots,3,
\end{equation}
where $S_k^{(j)}$ are the Stokes parameters of the ray $j$ in the laboratory frame, calculated from its Jones vector $(E_x,E_y)$ as $S_0=|E_x|^2+|E_y|^2$, $S_1=|E_x|^2-|E_y|^2$, $S_2=2\,\mathrm{Re}(E_xE_y^*)$ and $S_3=2\,\mathrm{Im}(E_x^*E_y)$,
with the conventions of \cite{SanchezdelRio2026efields} [time factor $e^{-i\omega t}$; $S_3>0$ for left-handed and $S_3<0$ for right-handed circular polarization]. The normalized Stokes parameters are $P_k=S_k/S_0$, and the degree of polarization is $\mathrm{DOP}=(P_1^2+P_2^2+P_3^2)^{1/2}$. Every ray is fully polarized, but the beam is partially polarized (DOP$<1$) when its rays have different polarization states. To compare with the measured $P_3$, we also calculate $\sqrt{1-P_1^2-P_2^2}$ from the simulated $P_1$ and $P_2$, as done with the measurements.

The simulations are done with the script \url{shadow4_desy_p09_scan.py}.

\subsection{Pencil beam}
\label{sec:pencil}

A pencil beam is a single ray, monochromatic (2838~eV) and with zero divergence, linearly polarized in the horizontal plane. It is equivalent to the calculation of the transmitted plane wave using the transmission amplitudes \cite{PolarizationDraft}, and it is used here as reference. The rocking scan, with a single ray traced for each detuning angle, is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:pencil}.

Far from the Bragg angle, the plate does not change the polarization ($P_1\simeq1$). Close to it, within the Darwin width, the plate acts as a polarizer, and the transmitted beam is linearly polarized along the $\pi$ direction of the plate ($P_2\simeq1$); the transmission drops to 0.011, about half of its value far from the Bragg angle (0.021 at the ends of the scan). On both sides of the reflection, the phase difference between the $\sigma$ and $\pi$ components produces circular polarization, with opposite handedness on each side: $|P_3|$ reaches 0.93 (right-handed, $P_3<0$) at $+31$~arcsec, and 0.72 (left-handed) at $-37$~arcsec (the handedness is predicted, not measured, see Sec.~\ref{sec:conclusions}). The largest circular polarization is obtained on the low-angle side of the reflection (positive measured angles), where the transmission is enhanced (up to 0.037).

The simulation reproduces the measured features, their positions, and the signs of $P_1$ and $P_2$. However, the calculated curves have sharper features than the measured ones, like the minimum $P_1=-0.96$ at $+17$~arcsec (the measured minimum is $P_1=-0.38$ at $+11$~arcsec). The root mean square (rms) differences with the 48 measured points are 0.15 for $P_1$ and 0.17 for $P_2$ (Table~\ref{tab:rms}).

\subsection{Monochromatic undulator beam}
\label{sec:undulator_mono}

The beam of the U32 undulator of P09 is simulated with the SHADOW4 undulator source, monochromatic at 2838~eV (first harmonic, deflection parameter $K=2.382$), with 5000 rays. The electron beam has the rms sizes and divergences of the high-$\beta$ section of PETRA III ($\sigma_x=\SI{141.5}{\micro\meter}$, $\sigma_y=\SI{4.9}{\micro\meter}$, $\sigma'_x=\SI{7.1}{\micro\radian}$, $\sigma'_y=\SI{2.1}{\micro\radian}$).
The rms divergences of the photon beam (convolution of the electron beam divergence and the natural divergence of the undulator radiation) are $11.2\times\SI{8.7}{\micro\radian}$ (H$\times$V). The rays are linearly polarized in the horizontal plane ($P_1=0.9999$).

The results are shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:undulator_mono}. They are very similar to those of the pencil beam (Table~\ref{tab:rms}), because the angular spread of the beam (about 2~arcsec rms in the diffraction plane of the plate) is much smaller than the Darwin width (half width 13.3~arcsec). The sharp features are slightly smoothed (the minimum of $P_1$ is $-0.84$), and the beam is slightly depolarized close to the Bragg angle (the minimum DOP is 0.78, at $+15$~arcsec), where $t_\sigma$ and $t_\pi$ change rapidly with the angle.

\subsection{Polychromatic undulator beam and Si 111 monochromator}
\label{sec:undulator_poly}

The beam incident on the phase plate is monochromatized by the Si 111 double-crystal monochromator of the beamline. The undulator source is now calculated in the energy interval 2837--2839~eV, and the beam is reflected by two Si 111 crystals (thick crystals, in the non-dispersive $(+,-)$ configuration, deflecting in the vertical plane), aligned for 2838~eV. As they deflect in the vertical plane, their $\sigma$ direction is horizontal and the polarization of the beam is practically not changed (as $\theta_B\simeq45^\circ$, their $\pi$ reflectivity is almost zero, and the small vertical component of the beam is removed). The energy distribution of the beam after the monochromator has a width of 0.27~eV FWHM (0.128~eV rms), as given by the square of the reflectivity curve of Si 111 at 2838~eV ($\Delta E/E=1.0\times10^{-4}$). At the phase plate, the energy spread is equivalent to an angular spread $\Delta\theta=(\Delta E/E)\tan\theta_B=\SI{94}{\micro\radian}$, or 19~arcsec FWHM, which is comparable to the Darwin width (full width \SI{129}{\micro\radian}, or 26.6~arcsec). This is in agreement with the estimation for P09 of an angular spread larger than 10~arcsec due to the energy spread at energies below 9~keV \cite{Strempfer2013}. The mean energy of the beam after the monochromator is 2837.96~eV, slightly lower than the energy used to align the phase plate (2838~eV); this offset of 0.04~eV shifts the curves by about 3~arcsec (its effect is discussed in Sec.~\ref{sec:convolution}).

Two settings of the undulator are considered, because the divergence of the polychromatic beam depends on the tuning of the undulator with respect to the working energy. For the undulator tuned at resonance at 2838~eV ($K=2.382$), the rms divergences of the photon beam are $11.0\times\SI{8.7}{\micro\radian}$ (H$\times$V), similar to the monochromatic case (with a maximum angular aperture of \SI{30}{\micro\radian}). When the undulator is ``blue-shifted'', with the resonance at 2868~eV ($K=2.365$), the working energy 2838~eV lies at the top of the spectral peak of the angle-integrated flux, and the rms divergences of the photon beam are larger, $14.0\times\SI{12.5}{\micro\radian}$ (H$\times$V); projected on the diffraction plane of the plate, they are 2.1 and 2.8~arcsec rms, respectively, much smaller than the angular spread due to the energy bandwidth. In both cases the rays are linearly polarized in the horizontal plane ($P_1=0.9999$).


The results are shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:undulator_poly}. The two settings of the undulator give practically the same results (Table~\ref{tab:rms}), as the angular spread at the phase plate is dominated by the energy bandwidth; the values given below are for the undulator at resonance. The energy bandwidth smooths the curves considerably, and the agreement with the measurements of $P_1$ and $P_2$ improves (rms differences 0.093 and 0.069, Table~\ref{tab:rms}): the minimum of $P_1$ is now $-0.50$ at $+10$~arcsec, and the maximum of $P_2$ is 0.95 at $-10$~arcsec. Some differences remain: the simulated minimum of $P_1$ is deeper than the measured one ($-0.38$), the simulated $P_1$ decreases about 10~arcsec earlier than the measured one on the side of negative angles (between about $-60$ and $-30$~arcsec), and the simulated $P_2$ close to $+30$~arcsec is $-0.33$, while the measured one is $-0.27$ (see Sec.~\ref{sec:conclusions} for the possible causes). The beam is now partially polarized close to the Bragg angle (the minimum DOP is 0.52, at $+7.5$~arcsec; 0.51 for the blue-shifted undulator). The maximum circular polarization is $|P_3|=0.83$ (right-handed, at $+35$~arcsec, with DOP$=0.93$ and a transmission of the plate of 0.033), and 0.68 (left-handed, at $-42.5$~arcsec) on the other side of the reflection.

The measured values of $\sqrt{1-P_1^2-P_2^2}$ (up to 0.97) are larger than the calculated $|P_3|$. This is not a disagreement: for a partially polarized beam, $\sqrt{1-P_1^2-P_2^2}$ includes the unpolarized part of the beam and overestimates $|P_3|$. When the same quantity is calculated from the simulated $P_1$ and $P_2$ (dashed line in Fig.~\ref{fig:undulator_poly}), it reproduces the measurements, including the minimum close to the Bragg angle (rms difference 0.068). This shows that the circular polarization of the beam cannot be obtained from the measured linear polarization alone when the beam is partially polarized, as is the case close to the Bragg angle.

\section{Approximated calculation via convolution}
\label{sec:convolution}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------
% -----------------------------------------------------------------

The results of Sec.~\ref{sec:results} show that the main effect of the beam incident on the phase plate is its angular spread, which comes mainly from the energy bandwidth of the monochromator. This suggests a simpler, approximated calculation: the Stokes parameters calculated for a plane wave are convolved with the distribution of the deviations from the Bragg angle of the incident beam.

For a plane wave with the standard Jones vector $\mathbf{E}_{in}$, the Jones vector of the transmitted beam, in the laboratory frame, is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:jones_plate}
\mathbf{E}_{out}(\Delta\theta)=R(-\chi)\begin{pmatrix} t_\sigma(\Delta\theta) & 0\\ 0 & t_\pi(\Delta\theta)\end{pmatrix}R(\chi)\,\mathbf{E}_{in},
\end{equation}
with $R(\chi)=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\chi&\sin\chi\\-\sin\chi&\cos\chi\end{pmatrix}$ the (passive) rotation matrix and $\chi$ the rotation angle of the plate around the beam \cite{PolarizationDraft}. The Stokes parameters $S_k(\Delta\theta)$ ($k=0,\dots,3$) of $\mathbf{E}_{out}$ are calculated as for a single ray in Eq.~(\ref{eq:stokes_sum}). They are calculated with the function {\tt calc\_xcrystal\_angular\_scan} of {\it crystalpy} \cite{Guigay2024}, which follows the conventions of \cite{PolarizationDraft} (time factor $e^{-i\omega t}$ and $S_3>0$ for left-handed polarization), the same as SHADOW4 \cite{SanchezdelRio2026efields}. In {\it crystalpy} the angle $\chi$ is measured counter-clockwise for an observer looking toward the source, the same sense as the angles of the polarization ellipse, while the orientation angle $\alpha$ of SHADOW4 is clockwise; therefore $\chi=-\alpha$. With $\mathbf{E}_{in}=(1,0)^T$ (horizontal polarization) and $\chi=-45^\circ$, the Stokes parameters coincide with those of the SHADOW4 pencil beam of Sec.~\ref{sec:pencil} with $\alpha=+45^\circ$.

The incident beam is an incoherent superposition of plane waves with different deviations $\Delta\theta'$ from the Bragg angle, with a distribution $g(\Delta\theta')$. As the Stokes parameters are additive for incoherent waves, the Stokes parameters of the transmitted beam are the convolution
\begin{equation}\label{eq:convolution}
\overline{S}_k(\Delta\theta)=\int S_k(\Delta\theta-\Delta\theta')\,g(\Delta\theta')\,d\Delta\theta', \qquad \overline{P}_k=\overline{S}_k/\overline{S}_0 .
\end{equation}
Note that the non-normalized Stokes parameters must be convolved, and not the normalized $P_k$. The convolved beam is partially polarized (DOP$<1$) where $S_k$ changes within the width of $g$.

We approximate $g$ by a Gaussian of rms width $\sigma$. A photon of energy $E$ is diffracted at the Bragg angle $\theta_B(E)$, so that an energy deviation $\delta E$ is equivalent to an angular deviation $-(\delta E/E)\tan\theta_B$. Using the values of Sec.~\ref{sec:undulator_poly}, the energy spread after the monochromator (0.128~eV rms) is equivalent to 9.0~arcsec rms, and the divergence of the undulator beam projected on the diffraction plane of the plate is 2.1~arcsec rms, so that $\sigma=(9.0^2+2.1^2)^{1/2}=9.2$~arcsec. \todo{FWHM or rms? the energy distribution after the DCM is not Gaussian (0.28~eV FWHM)}

The result is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:convolution}. The agreement with the measurements is good, with rms differences 0.067 for $P_1$, 0.040 for $P_2$ and 0.056 for $\sqrt{1-P_1^2-P_2^2}$ (Table~\ref{tab:rms}). The rms differences are minimal for $\sigma\simeq9.5$--10.5~arcsec (Fig.~\ref{fig:convolution_rms}), close to the value estimated from the beam. The agreement is better than with the SHADOW4 simulation of Sec.~\ref{sec:undulator_poly}. The difference comes mainly from the mean energy of the beam after the monochromator (2837.96~eV), which shifts the SHADOW4 curves by about 3~arcsec with respect to the convolved ones (centered at 2838~eV): when the convolved curves are shifted by the same amount, the rms differences become 0.092, 0.074 and 0.076, close to those of SHADOW4. The remaining differences come from the shape of the energy distribution after the monochromator, which is not Gaussian. \todo{check the sign of the shift; discuss whether the 2838~eV alignment of the plate should use the mean energy}

The convolution is a fast and simple approximation, useful to analyze the measurements, e.g., to fit the width of the angular distribution. It assumes that the distribution of the angular deviations is the same at all the angles of the scan, and that the energy dependence of $t_\sigma$ and $t_\pi$ is only through the deviation from the Bragg angle. The ray tracing does not need these approximations, and it includes the actual distributions of the beam (angular, spatial, spectral, and their correlations), the optical elements of the beamline, and the alignment of all the elements.

The calculations are done with the script {\tt crystalpy\_desy\_p09\_convolution.py}.

\section{Discussion and conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------
% -----------------------------------------------------------------

The SHADOW4 phase plate element, with the polarization of the rays transported by the Jones calculus, reproduces the polarization of the beam transmitted by Si 111 phase plates (\SI{10}{\micro\meter} and \SI{5}{\micro\meter} thick) measured at the P09 beamline of PETRA III. A calculation with a plane monochromatic wave (or a pencil beam) reproduces the positions of the features of the rocking scan (and, with the rotation of the plate and the sign of the detuning determined in Sec.~\ref{sec:simulation}, the signs of $P_1$ and $P_2$), but not their amplitudes. The angular distribution of the undulator beam has little effect, because it is much smaller than the Darwin width of the reflection. The energy bandwidth of the Si 111 monochromator, which corresponds to an angular spread at the phase plate comparable to the Darwin width, is the main cause of the smoothing of the measured curves, and must be included to reproduce the measurements; the tuning of the undulator (at resonance or blue-shifted) has little effect. The convolution of the plane-wave Stokes parameters with a Gaussian of the same angular width is a fast approximation that gives a similar agreement, and it can be used to analyze the measurements: fitting its width gives an estimate of the effective angular spread of the beam at the phase plate, $\sigma\simeq9.5$--10.5~arcsec rms for the \SI{10}{\micro\meter} plate, close to the 9.2~arcsec estimated from the beam; the ray tracing, however, does not need its assumptions, and it includes the actual distributions of the beam and all the elements of the beamline.

For the \SI{10}{\micro\meter} plate and the beam after the monochromator, the maximum circular polarization is $|P_3|=0.83$ at $+35$~arcsec, with DOP$=0.93$ and a transmission of the plate of 0.033; it is obtained on the low-angle side of the reflection, where the transmission is enhanced.

The simulations give access to quantities that are not measured. The handedness of the circular polarization is predicted, provided that the sense of the rotation of the plate and the sign convention of the measured $P_2$ coincide with those used here (Sec.~\ref{sec:simulation} and below); with these conventions, the beam is right-handed on the side of positive measured angles (the low-angle side of the reflection, where the circular polarization is largest) and left-handed on the other side. The degree of polarization of the transmitted beam is also obtained, showing that the beam is partially polarized close to the Bragg angle. In this case, the circular polarization obtained from the measured linear polarization assuming a fully polarized beam is overestimated, and the simulated $|P_3|$ is lower than the measured $\sqrt{1-P_1^2-P_2^2}$.

The remaining differences may be due to the parameters of the simulation (in particular the actual bandwidth and alignment of the monochromator, including the offset of 0.04~eV of the mean energy after the monochromator, which shifts the curves by about 3~arcsec, Sec.~\ref{sec:convolution}), to the zero and scale of the measured angle axis, to the crystal quality and thickness of the phase plate, to the polarization analyzer, and to the uncertainty of the sense of rotation of the plate and of the angle scan, which were deduced here from the measurements.

\paragraph{Measurements with a \SI{5}{\micro\meter} plate.} Measurements with a thinner Si 111 plate, of thickness \SI{5}{\micro\meter}, at the same photon energy and with the same setup, are also available, with a lower statistical accuracy (uncertainties of about 0.08 in $P_1$ and $P_2$, instead of 0.02 for the \SI{10}{\micro\meter} plate). They are compared in Fig.~\ref{fig:5um} with the SHADOW4 simulation of the polychromatic beam of the blue-shifted undulator after the monochromator, and with the convolution of Sec.~\ref{sec:convolution} with the same width ($\sigma=9.2$~arcsec). The plate is more transparent (transmission 0.15 far from the Bragg angle), and the features of the rocking scan are narrower and closer to the Bragg angle. Both calculations reproduce the measurements, with rms differences of 0.096 ($P_1$), 0.116 ($P_2$) and 0.084 ($\sqrt{1-P_1^2-P_2^2}$) for SHADOW4, and 0.069, 0.074 and 0.062 for the convolution, comparable to the uncertainties of the measurements. As for the \SI{10}{\micro\meter} plate, the difference between the two calculations comes mainly from the shift of a few arcsec due to the mean energy of the beam after the monochromator, which is more important here as the features are narrower (when the convolved curves are shifted by 3~arcsec, the rms differences become 0.090, 0.112 and 0.080, close to those of SHADOW4). The maximum circular polarization is $|P_3|=0.78$ (right-handed, at $+17.5$~arcsec, with DOP$=0.86$). In both calculations the features are somewhat sharper than in the measurements (minimum of $P_1$ and maximum of $P_2$), and the width of the Gaussian that best fits the measurements is larger than for the \SI{10}{\micro\meter} plate (11--16~arcsec), which could indicate an additional angular spread or imperfections of the plate, although the statistical accuracy of these measurements does not allow a firm conclusion.

\paragraph{Open points for discussion.} 
\begin{itemize}
\item {\it Sign conventions of the measurement.} The sense of the measured angle and of the rotation of the plate are not known in the SHADOW4 frame. They were deduced by requiring the simulated signs of $P_1$ and $P_2$ to agree with the measured ones: among $\alpha=\pm45^\circ$ and the two senses of the angle axis, only $\alpha=+45^\circ$ with a detuning equal to minus the measured angle does it (Table~\ref{tab:combinations}). This must be confirmed with the geometry of the experiment.
\item {\it Sign of $P_2$ and handedness.} In the simulation, $P_2=+1$ corresponds to an electric field along $\hat{x}-\hat{z}$ (pointing down and to the right for an observer at the source looking downstream), because the laboratory basis $(\hat{x},-\hat{z})$ is right-handed with the beam along $\hat{y}$ (with $\hat{z}$ pointing up). A mirror reflection of the whole setup (left--right) changes the signs of both $P_2$ and $P_3$, but not of $P_1$. Therefore, the predicted handedness follows from the sign of the measured $P_2$, and it is reversed if the convention used at P09 for the $+45^\circ$ direction is the opposite one. The handedness itself is not measured, because $P_3$ is obtained as $+\sqrt{1-P_1^2-P_2^2}$.
\item {\it Measured $P_3$.} As $\sqrt{1-P_1^2-P_2^2}$ assumes a fully polarized beam, it must be compared with the same quantity calculated from the simulated $P_1$ and $P_2$, not with the simulated $|P_3|$ (Sec.~\ref{sec:undulator_poly}).
\item {\it Electron beam.} The electron beam parameters are the PETRA III design values in a high-$\beta$ section ($\varepsilon_x\simeq1$~nm\,rad, $\varepsilon_y\simeq10$~pm\,rad). Differences in these values have little effect here, because the angular spread of the beam is much smaller than the Darwin width.
\item {\it Energy bandwidth.} The bandwidth is calculated with an ideal Si 111 double-crystal monochromator (thick, unstrained crystals, perfectly aligned, without heat load): 0.27~eV FWHM (0.128~eV rms), as given by the square of the reflectivity curve of Si 111. The convolution of Sec.~\ref{sec:convolution} gives an independent estimate of the effective angular spread at the phase plate from the measurements, consistent with this bandwidth. The actual bandwidth of the monochromator of P09 at 2838~eV should be confirmed, because it is the main parameter of the comparison. The electron energy spread (0.1\%) is not applied to the undulator emission; it does not change the bandwidth selected by the monochromator.
\item {\it Alignment.} In the simulations, the monochromator and the phase plate (plus the detuning) are aligned for 2838~eV. The mean energy of the beam after the monochromator is 2837.96~eV, which shifts the curves by about 3~arcsec and changes the rms differences with the measurements (Sec.~\ref{sec:convolution}). How were the monochromator and the zero of the angle scale of the phase plate set in the experiment? The zero of the measured angle scale is assumed to coincide with the Bragg angle for 2838~eV; no shift or scale factor has been fitted.
\item {\it Phase plate.} The plate is assumed to be a perfect, unstrained Si 111 crystal of exactly \SI{10}{\micro\meter} (or \SI{5}{\micro\meter}), in symmetric Bragg geometry. Strain, thickness variations or a miscut would broaden or shift the features.
\item {\it Other measurements.} The measurements with the \SI{5}{\micro\meter} plate have a lower statistical accuracy; more accurate measurements with this or other thicknesses would allow checking the assumptions on the angular spread of the beam and on the thickness and crystal quality of the plate.
\item {\it Polarization analyzer.} The measured $P_1$ and $P_2$ are taken as exact; the finite extinction of the analyzer and other instrumental effects are not considered.
\item {\it Phase plates at P09.} The description of the phase plates of P09 (diamond for 3.2--14~keV, \SI{10}{\micro\meter} Si for 2.7--3.2~keV) is taken from \cite{Strempfer2013}, where some of them are described as planned; the present equipment should be confirmed.
\end{itemize}
